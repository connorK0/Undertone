CHAPTER ONE
The Lamplighter's Round

The morning came in grey and unhurried over Ashgate, the way it had come for
forty years, and Elias Vance walked his round with the ladder on his shoulder
and the taper cold in his hand. There was no need to light anything at this
hour. He walked it anyway. The stones were wet and the gulls were arguing over
the fish quay, and somewhere behind the customs house a woman was singing while
she beat a rug.

He liked the hour before the town was properly awake. The baker's boy nodded to
him. The girl who kept the flower stall was setting out her buckets, and she
lifted a hand, and he lifted his, and neither of them said anything, which was
how they had greeted each other for eleven years.

At the top of Cathedral Street he stopped, as he always stopped, and looked down
the length of it toward the water. Forty lamps. He knew every one of them by the
particular way it sulked in bad weather.

It was there, at the twelfth lamp, that he saw the door of the Hollis house
standing open.

Not ajar. Open, wide, pushed back flat against the wall, the way a door is left
when someone has gone through it fast and has not thought about it since. The
hall beyond was dark. There was no sound from inside. On the step, quite neatly,
as though it had been set down rather than dropped, was a single shoe.

Elias stood in the street with the ladder still on his shoulder and felt the
morning go quiet around him. Not the true quiet -- the gulls were still at it,
the woman was still singing behind the customs house -- but the other kind, the
kind that happens inside a man's ears when some part of him has understood
something before the rest of him has caught up.

He set the ladder down against the railing. He did not call out. Afterward he
could never explain why he did not call out, only that calling out had seemed,
in that moment, like the worst thing a person could possibly do.

The hall smelled of cold ash and something underneath it that he did not have a
word for. He went in three steps and stopped. The stair rail was broken at the
fourth baluster, snapped clean, the raw wood showing pale as bone. Something had
gone over it, or been put over it. Above him a floorboard shifted its weight,
slowly, deliberately, the way a board does when someone standing on it is trying
very hard not to be heard, and Elias understood with perfect clarity that he was
not alone in the house and that whoever was upstairs had been listening to him
since the moment he came through the door.

He did not run. He backed out. One step, then another, his eyes on the dark at
the top of the stair, and the floorboard did not shift again, and that was
somehow worse than if it had. When his heel found the step outside he turned and
he went down Cathedral Street faster than he had gone anywhere in twenty years,
past the twelfth lamp and the eleventh and the tenth, and he did not look back
until he had reached the sergeant's door and was hammering on it with the flat
of his hand.

They found the Hollis boy in the cellar by nine o'clock. They found nothing else
at all -- no forced window, no marks in the yard, and, though four men went
through the house from the slates to the foundations, no one upstairs and no way
that anyone upstairs could have got out.

Elias sat on the low wall across the street for a long while after they had gone
in, holding his cold taper, watching the door. Someone brought him tea and he
held that too and did not drink it.

He thought about the shoe on the step, set down so neatly. He thought about how
a person carrying a boy down to a cellar might stop, in the middle of it, and
take the trouble to place a shoe upright on a doorstep. He turned it over and
over and could make nothing of it, and the turning of it was the beginning of
the thirty years that came after.

That night he lit his forty lamps as he had always lit them, beginning at the
water and working up. When he came to the twelfth he stood under it longer than
he needed to. The flame caught, and steadied, and threw his shadow up the wall of
the Hollis house, very tall and very thin, and he thought: I will be the one who
finds out. Not the sergeant. Not the men from the county. Me. I walk this street
twice a day and I know every stone of it and I will be the one.

He was sixty-one years old. He had never in his life done anything that anyone
would have called remarkable, and he did not particularly expect to start now,
and he made the promise anyway, standing in the lamplight with the ladder on his
shoulder, to a boy he had never spoken to and a street that was not listening.
